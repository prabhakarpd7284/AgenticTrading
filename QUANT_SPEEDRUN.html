How to speedrun a quant trader career with AI

A speedrun in gaming means completing the game in minimum time using every legitimate optimisation the system allows — not cheats, not shortcuts that break the experience, just deep system knowledge plus tools that turn dead time into progress. This guide applies the same frame to becoming a quant trader. The "skips" are AI tools that compress months of solo grinding into days of guided learning.

2 · What a quant trader actually is

Four roles in the quant industry that often get confused:

RoleWhat they doWho fits
Quant TraderMakes trade decisions · market making · risk managementSTEM bachelor's with olympiad background · fast reactions
Quant ResearcherFinds alpha signals · builds modelsPhD/Master's · research mindset
Quant DeveloperInfrastructure · low-latency systemsStrong SWE background (C++/Rust)
Quant AnalystDerivatives pricing · risk (sell-side)Strong stochastic calculus

Why the trader role is the most accessible

  • A STEM bachelor's is enough. Jane Street explicitly states "prior knowledge of finance or economics is not expected or required." Two-thirds of their intern class is CS and math, not finance.
  • No PhD gating — unlike research positions at Renaissance, Two Sigma, D.E. Shaw.
  • Highest variance career path. Exceptional traders make 8 figures; ordinary ones $200–400 k entry, $800 k – 1.2 M after 5 years.
  • Most "real-time mathematical" role — probability puzzles, mental math, decisions under pressure.

What a trader does day-to-day

  • Market making — quotes bid/ask, manages inventory, hedges risk.
  • Implements strategies built by researchers.
  • Makes fast decisions: pricing nuances, order book dynamics, rare events.
  • Manages P&L on their book in real time.
  • Pairs with researchers to improve models from live data.
Core frame. A trader doesn't predict the market. A trader finds situations where the mathematical probability is shifted in their favour, sizes the position correctly, and repeats the process thousands of times until EV accumulates into real returns. It's a casino from the other side of the table.

5 · Step 1 — Math foundation

The longest and most unforgiving step. At an interview at Jane Street or Optiver it shows in 5 minutes whether you went through this honestly or jumped past it. Five layers, in strict order:

   Stochastic calculus       ← Black–Scholes, asset price modelling
              ▲
   Calculus + optimisation   ← portfolio construction
              ▲
   Linear algebra            ← covariance, PCA, factors
              ▲
   Statistics                ← hypothesis testing, regression
              ▲
   Probability               ← the heart of the profession
1.1

Probability

8–12 weeks

The most important layer. Everything reduces to one question: what are the odds, and are they in my favour?

Conditional thinking.

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

Unconditional probability the market moves sharply after an economic announcement is 40%. Conditional on options implied vol being elevated beforehand, the probability becomes 68%. Those 28 percentage points are real alpha.

Bayes' theorem. Traders who update beliefs faster and more accurately consistently outperform.

Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) / Evidence

Expected value and variance. Two numbers you'll think about for the rest of your career. Positive EV + survivable variance = money. Position too large relative to variance = bankruptcy before EV gets a chance to work.

Resources

  • Blitzstein & Hwang — Introduction to Probability (Harvard, free) — ch. 1–6
  • Harvard Stat 110 (Joe Blitzstein) — best probability course in the world, full YouTube playlist
  • DeGroot & Schervish — Probability and Statistics (the standard)
1.2

Statistics

4–6 weeks

Probability is theory. Statistics is the practice of listening to data. The main lesson: most things that look like signal are actually noise.

  • Hypothesis testing. You've built a strategy, backtest shows 15% annual returns. Real edge or luck? If you tested 1,000 random strategies, 50 of them will show a "significant" result at 5% by pure chance. This is the multiple-comparisons problem — the main reason backtests look great and live results are terrible.
  • Linear regression. Regress strategy returns on known risk factors. If alpha == 0 after accounting for all factors, your "edge" is hidden exposure to something already known.

Resource

  • Wasserman — All of Statistics (fast and dense), ch. 1–13
1.3

Linear algebra

4–6 weeks

Portfolio construction, PCA, neural networks, covariance matrices.

σ²(portfolio) = wᵀ Σ w

Heart of Markowitz portfolio theory. Eigenvalues show what actually matters. In 500 stocks, the first 5 eigenvectors explain ~70% of all variance. The rest is noise. This is the foundation of factor investing.

Resources

  • Gilbert Strang — MIT 18.06 (OCW free) + Introduction to Linear Algebra
  • 3Blue1Brown — Essence of Linear Algebra (visual intuition)
1.4

Calculus + optimisation

4–5 weeks

Almost every problem in quant finance reduces to maximise X subject to constraints. Key idea: a convex problem has a unique global solution that can be found efficiently.

Resource

  • Boyd & Vandenberghe — Convex Optimization (Stanford, free), ch. 1–5
  • Stanford EE364A (Boyd's course)
1.5

Stochastic calculus

6–8 weeks

After this layer you can derive how financial instruments are priced from first principles. Central idea: in a world with randomness, the square of a small random increment is not negligible. This produces Itô's lemma — the chain rule of stochastic calculus. Apply it to an option price:

∂V/∂t + ½ σ² S² (∂²V/∂S²) + r S (∂V/∂S) − r V = 0

What's remarkable: the expected return of the stock disappears completely. The option price doesn't depend on where you think the stock is going. Only on how much it moves.

Resource

  • Steven Shreve — Stochastic Calculus for Finance II · industry standard
How AI changes the math. Before: stuck on a Shreve proof → days alone, forums, often give up. Now → 30-second prompt:
I'm stuck on a proof: [text] My level: I know definitions through chapter X. Explain this step three ways: 1. Formally with definitions 2. Geometrically or through intuition 3. With a concrete numerical example Then give me 3 increasingly difficult exercises for comprehension.
Same applies to probability paradoxes, regression derivations, eigenvector intuition.

6 · Step 2 — Programming

In parallel with math, not sequentially. Two different worlds, often confused:

Research programming

Python, clean code, statistics, ML. The trader's and researcher's main tool.

Production programming

C++ or Rust, microsecond latency. For quant developers; for traders, basic understanding is a plus.

Python for traders

LibraryWhy
NumPyVectorisation — 10–100× faster than ordinary Python
PandasTime series, multi-index, groupby
PolarsModern Pandas replacement, 10–50× faster on large datasets. Must-have in 2026.
SciPyStatistics, optimisation
statsmodelsStatistical tests
cvxpyConvex optimisation
xgboost · lightgbmTabular ML

Resource. Wes McKinney — Python for Data Analysis (from the author of Pandas, free online).

SQL + big data

Joins, window functions, CTEs, parquet handling, basic ClickHouse (standard for financial time-series). Resources: Mode Analytics SQL Tutorial, LeetCode SQL.

C++ / Rust

For a trader: not critical, but a plus. C++ has been the HFT standard for decades. Rust is gaining ground — NautilusTrader (open-source production-grade trading platform) uses a Rust core + Python API; the pattern is becoming standard.

How AI changes programming. This is the most AI-transformed area in the whole roadmap.
  • Code review. Previously needed a senior. Now: "here's my backtester, find look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, where transaction costs are wrong" — answer in a minute, more accurate than half the seniors.
  • Debugging. Ask for "5 hypotheses for why this test fails" instead of "fix the bug." Forces you to think, accelerates massively.
  • Reproducing algorithms. Academic paper → pseudocode → implementation. Used to take weeks. Now an evening.
  • Boilerplate. Data loading, viz, configs — 30 seconds via prompt. Frees you for the core logic.

7 · Step 3 — Finance & market structure

STEM candidates are often weakest here. They arrive with strong math but don't understand option Greeks or yield curves.

Foundations

  • Hull — Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives. The main book. Everyone reads it.
  • Bodie, Kane, Marcus — Investments (standard textbook)
  • Time value of money · NPV · bond pricing · duration · MPT (Markowitz) · CAPM · Fama–French

Options & volatility (trader-specific)

  • Natenberg — Option Volatility and Pricing
  • The Greeks (delta, gamma, vega, theta, rho) — interpret and use
  • Implied vol · vol surface · smile / skew
  • Put-call parity · exotic options (conceptually)

Market microstructure (critical for traders)

  • Cartea, Jaimungal, Penalva — Algorithmic and High-Frequency Trading
  • Order-book mechanics · market makers · liquidity
  • Bid-ask spread · market impact · adverse selection
  • Order types and how to use them

Industry understanding

  • Rishi Narang — Inside the Black Box (how quant funds are built)
  • Scott Patterson — The Quants (industry documentary)
  • Greg Zuckerman — The Man Who Solved the Market (Renaissance)
How AI changes this. Finance literature is dense and opaque. A prompt:
Explain the [theta hedging] concept in Hull chapter X. Level: I know Black–Scholes, don't understand how to apply in practice. Give me: 1) intuition 2) concrete numeric example 3) common mistakes
breaks down a chapter in an evening that previously took a week. Especially powerful for papers:
Attached paper [PDF]. I want to reproduce it for portfolio. Background: [background]. 1. Key idea in 3 paragraphs 2. Math I need to know 3. What data is needed and where to get it 4. Algorithm pseudocode 5. What simulated data to verify correctness 6. Main implementation pitfalls (look-ahead bias) 7. How to visualise results well
Previously a serious paper was a week-long task. Now an hour.

LLM for financial data

  • Chunking strategies for financial docs

  • Confidence scoring

Agent workflows

  • Task decomposition
  • Tool use (LLM calls Python/SQL/APIs)
  • Validation loops (one agent critiques another)
  • Human-in-the-loop checkpoints

AI risk & model validation

  • Overfitting checks on financial data
  • Look-ahead bias in LLM-generated code
  • Interpretability (XAI for regulators)
  • Black-box problem in practice
Build something real — Minimal AlphaGPT clone. A 3-agent system you can build in a weekend with the Claude API:
  • Agent 1 · generates hypotheses in free form ("mean reversion in morning hours for small-caps")
  • Agent 2 · writes backtest code on historical data
  • Agent 3 · critiques the result — overfitting, leakage, transaction costs
This is the single most impressive project you can show a Citadel recruiter in 2026.

10 · Step 6 — Interview prep

Its own genre. You can know everything and still fail interviews. Citadel Super Days are 6 consecutive 45-minute interviews — probability proofs, coding, behavioural.

Probability puzzles & brainteasers

  • Xinfeng Zhou — A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews (the Green Book). 200+ real problems.
  • Timothy Crack — Heard on the Street
  • Mark Joshi — Quant Job Interview Questions and Answers
  • brainstellar.com — interview-difficulty problems with solutions

Mental math (critical, not optional)

Trainer: Zetamac.

Goal: 50+ correct answers/min before you start applying. Without this you can't pass trader interviews at Optiver / IMC / Jane Street.

Coding interviews

LeetCode medium-hard. Especially: arrays, dynamic programming, graphs. DP is the most common failure point in final rounds at Citadel and Jane Street.

Often asked: implement a numerical algorithm — Black–Scholes, Monte Carlo, optimisation.

Behavioural · Jane Street specifics

Seriously underestimated. Jane Street deliberately makes problems too hard for one person — they watch how you use hints, reason under pressure, take criticism. A candidate who narrates their thinking and acknowledges uncertainty consistently outperforms one who goes silent.

How AI changes preparation. Biggest acceleration anywhere in the roadmap.
Play a senior quant trader at Citadel. Conduct a final-round interview for an entry-level trader position. Structure (1 hour): 1. Probability puzzle (5–10 min) — no hints until requested 2. Mental math (rapid fire, 2 min) 3. Market-making case study (15 min) 4. Coding on numerical algorithm (15 min) 5. Behavioural (10 min) Don't give hints without request. At the end — feedback: what's strong, weak, what to brush up on.
Previously: find a partner, schedule, one hour a week. Now: an hour every day with infinite question variation.

12 · Main mistakes

  1. Skipping math. Visible at interview in 5 minutes.
  2. Ignoring mental math. Trader interviews without 50+ Zetamac are impossible to pass.
  3. Toy projects. Backtests on S&P without costs, without realistic execution. Zero résumé value.
  4. Not entering competitions. Most direct path, yet many ignore it.
  5. Ignoring the AI step. In 2026 top firms won't hire without it. Learn it in the process, not "later."
  6. Ignoring soft skills. Technical people with zero communication don't pass final rounds.
  7. Not applying early. "Getting fully ready" is forever. After 5–10 interviews you learn more about your gaps than after half a year of theory.
  8. Using AI wrong. Don't use it → lose 5–10× acceleration. Over-rely → never learn to think. AI accelerates learning, but you still have to do the thinking.
